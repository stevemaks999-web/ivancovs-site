The ISO 7730 thermal comfort standard in practice

[/vc_column_text][vc_column_text]Topic 1: Theory versus reality — can the mean rating (PMV) describe individual comfort?

PMV predicts the mean rating for a large group of people. How should you handle situations where people with very different metabolic levels work in the same room (for example, an office where both an office employee and a courier who often comes and goes work)?

Does the standard take sufficient account of psychological factors (for example, the effect of clothing choice and personal preferences on perceived temperature)? Is it always possible and necessary to aim for PMV = 0?

Example from real life: women complain about the cold in offices more often than men. How can this difference be reduced by following the recommendations of the standard?

 

Topic 2: Energy saving versus maximum comfort — where is the balance point?

ISO 7730 offers four comfort categories (I, II, III, IV). Which category would you choose, and why, for different buildings (for example, a luxury hotel, a public authority, a factory, a school)?

What energy-efficiency gain is possible by lowering the requirements from category I to category II? Is this compromise justified?

How can the standard help to “redesign” heating and cooling systems so that they do not run at maximum load all the time but only maintain an acceptable comfort level?

 

Topic 3: Local discomfort — do we pay enough attention to it?

The standard describes in detail the causes of local discomfort (thermal radiation, draught, vertical and horizontal temperature asymmetry, hot/cold ceilings and floors). Which of these problems is most common in buildings in Latvia?

How can you effectively deal with thermal radiation from windows or ventilation diffusers without closing them completely and without spoiling air quality?

Discuss how rooms with large glazed areas (façades) create challenges for ensuring local thermal comfort (radiant temperature, asymmetry). What solutions are possible?

 

Topic 4: Dynamic conditions — is the standard too “static”?

ISO 7730 mainly considers a stabilised, static state. In real life, however, conditions change (solar intensity, number of people in the room). How can systems be adapted to these changes based on the basic principles of the standard?

Does the adaptive thermal comfort theory (that people get used to and adapt to seasonal changes) contradict ISO 7730? Should different indoor temperatures be applied in summer and winter?

What role do air humidity (relative humidity) and its control play in achieving comfort? Does the standard give it enough weight?

 

Topic 5: The standard as a tool in the design and maintenance stages

How can PMV/PPD calculations be used in practice at the design stage to choose suitable heating, cooling and ventilation equipment? What software tools are available for this?

How can the standard help a maintenance specialist or energy auditor assess the performance of an existing building and identify problems? What simple measurements (air velocity, temperature at different heights) can give clues?

Are individual workplace controls based on PMV/PPD principles (personal air jets, heaters) a good solution, or do they disrupt the operation of the central system?

 

Topic 6: Ethics, sustainability and future challenges

Is it ethical to design and build buildings that only minimally meet the lowest comfort category (C) in order to save on construction costs? What is the lowest acceptable level for public spaces?

How does the concept of thermal comfort fit into the broader context of sustainable construction? Are “comfort” and “sustainability” in conflict, or can they exist in harmony?

How will thermal comfort requirements and their management change in the future, taking into account climate change, remote work and advancing technologies (for example, AI-controlled HVAC systems)?[/vc_column_text][vc_empty_space height="20px"][/vc_column_inner][/vc_row_inner][/verticaltab][/verticaltabs][/vc_column][/vc_row]
